My ankle was swollen to nearly twice its normal size, the skin darkening to a deep purple-blue that was visible even in the moonlight. She probed it with her fingers, carefully, and I gripped the tree roots hard enough to leave nail marks in the bark.

"Not broken," she said. "Badly sprained. Maybe a torn ligament. The calf is worse."

She unwrapped the bandage. The splinter wound was ugly — a ragged puncture in the meat of the muscle, deep enough that the edges gaped when she pressed around it. Something was still in there. I could feel it — a foreign body, a piece of that rotten timber, sitting inside my calf like a sliver of malice.

"There's still wood in the wound," she said. "I can't get it out here. Not in the dark, not with dirty hands. If it stays in, it'll fester."

"Then we find a healer."

"In Valdmere? At this hour? The temple healers won't see you without a donation we can't afford, and the apothecary on Mill Street closes at sunset."

"There has to be someone."

Mira was quiet for a moment. The forest dripped around us, patient and indifferent to our problems.

"There's someone," she said, slowly, in the tone of someone choosing their words the way you'd choose footing in a minefield. "In the Undercity. A woman I've heard about. She operates out of the old cistern district near the Lowgate."

"You've *heard about* her."

"From other sellswords. Mercenaries. People who get hurt at inconvenient hours and can't afford temple prices."

"What kind of healer operates out of a cistern at midnight?"

"The kind who doesn't ask questions and doesn't charge much. Her name is **Ysolde**. They call her the Cistern Witch."

"That's not reassuring."

"No one calls her that to her face. Look—" Mira crouched in front of me, her amber eyes catching the moonlight. "You've got a piece of rotten wood buried in your calf. If we wait until morning, you'll have an infection. A bad one. The kind that takes legs. I've seen it happen — twice. Both times, the man waited too long. First man lost the leg at the knee. Second man lost it at the hip, and then lost the rest of himself three days later."

I looked at my leg. The swelling was getting worse. The edges of the wound were hot to the touch — unnaturally hot, like the flesh around it was already fighting something.

"Fine," I said. "The Cistern Witch."

"Ysolde."

"Whatever. Take me there."

---

Getting back to Valdmere took longer than getting out. Mira half-carried me the entire way, my arm over her shoulder, her arm around my waist, both of us stumbling through the dark forest like the world's worst three-legged race. My leg throbbed with every step. The bleeding had slowed but not stopped, leaving a trail of dark drops on the wet leaves behind us.

We entered the city through a different gate — the south entrance, near the river, where the guards were fewer and the bribes were cheaper. Mira dropped a copper into the night guard's hand without breaking stride, and he looked the other way with the practiced disinterest of a man who'd seen far worse come through his gate at far worse hours.

The Undercity wasn't a separate city — it was the part of Valdmere that the rest of Valdmere pretended didn't exist. The oldest district, built on top of even older ruins, where the streets narrowed to alley-widths and the buildings leaned inward like conspirators sharing secrets. It was where the city's less reputable residents conducted their less reputable business: black-market traders, unlicensed alchemists, fortune tellers, and apparently, late-night healers with unfortunate nicknames.

The cistern district was the lowest point of the Undercity — literally. The old stone cisterns that had once served as the city's water supply had been drained, sealed, and forgotten centuries ago, and the buildings that had grown up over them sagged and shifted as the ground beneath them slowly settled. The streets here were perpetually damp, water seeping up through cracks in the cobblestones like the earth itself was sweating.

Mira stopped in front of a door.

It was unremarkable — wooden, weathered, set into a stone wall between a shuttered chandler's shop and a building with no signage that smelled strongly of fermenting something. No marking, no sign, no indication that anything medical or magical happened behind it. Just a door.

She knocked. Three times, then twice, then once. A pattern.

Nothing happened for a long moment. Then I heard footsteps — soft, measured, unhurried — approaching from the other side. A bolt drew back. A chain rattled. The door opened inward.

The woman standing in the doorway was not what I expected.

I'd been picturing — well, a "cistern witch." Hunched. Old. Possibly covered in warts. The kind of woman who'd have a black cat and a cauldron and would cackle at inappropriate moments.

**Ysolde** was none of those things. She was tall — nearly my height — and thin, with the angular, austere beauty of a woman who'd been striking in her youth and had hardened into something more interesting with age. Fifty, maybe. Maybe older. Her hair was iron-gray, pulled back from her face in a severe braid that hung over one shoulder. Her eyes were dark — so dark that in the lamplight from inside, I couldn't distinguish iris from pupil. They moved over me with the quick, assessing gaze of someone who saw injured bodies so regularly that the diagnosis was already happening before I opened my mouth.

She wore a simple linen dress under a leather apron stained with things I didn't want to identify. Her hands — long-fingered, steady, surprisingly elegant — hung at her sides. On her left wrist, she wore a bracelet of small glass vials, each one containing a different colored liquid. They clinked softly when she moved, like tiny wind chimes.

"The leg," she said. It wasn't a question.

"Splinter wound," Mira said. "Deep. There's still wood in the muscle. And the ankle — sprained, possibly torn."

Ysolde looked at Mira, then at me. "Bring him in."

---

The inside of Ysolde's... practice? Workshop? Lair? — was a single large room that had clearly once been a cellar and had been converted into something between a healer's clinic and an alchemist's laboratory. Stone walls, low ceiling, the faint sound of water running somewhere beneath the floor — we were close to the old cisterns here, maybe directly above them.

The room was lit by oil lamps and beeswax candles, the light warm and steady. Shelves lined every wall, packed with glass bottles, ceramic jars, bundles of dried herbs, rolled bandages, and instruments that ranged from the medical to the obscure. A heavy wooden table sat in the center of the room, scarred and stained with use, and beside it stood a smaller table covered with a white cloth and arranged with tools — forceps, needles, thread, scissors, a small bone saw that I very much hoped was decorative.

The smell was better than I expected — herbs, mostly. Lavender and sage and something sharp and clean that cut through the ambient damp. Not unpleasant. Professional.

Mira helped me onto the central table. The wood was cold against my back through my damp clothes. Ysolde moved around the room with efficient, unhurried grace, gathering things — a basin of water, strips of clean linen, a small leather case that she unrolled to reveal a set of delicate surgical instruments.

"Trousers," she said.

"What?"

"Remove them. Or I'll cut them off. Your choice."

Mira helped me peel the trousers down over my swollen ankle, a process that involved enough suppressed screaming to power a small theatre production. The calf wound was worse than it had looked in the forest — the skin around it was angry red, the edges swollen and hot, and a thin line of something yellowish was already seeping from the deepest part.

Ysolde examined it with her fingers. Her touch was firm but not rough — the practiced hands of someone who'd done this ten thousand times. She pressed around the wound, probing, and I gripped the edges of the table.

"Deep," she murmured. "Approximately two inches of foreign material still embedded. The wood has begun to splinter inside the wound track." She looked up at me, her dark eyes unreadable. "This will be unpleasant."

"More unpleasant than getting it was?"

"Possibly."

She turned to a shelf and selected three bottles — one clear, one amber, one a deep, unsettling green. She poured the clear liquid over the wound first. It burned — a clean, antiseptic burn that made me hiss through my teeth. Then she opened the leather case and selected a pair of long, thin forceps.

"Hold still," she said. "And try not to scream. My neighbors are already irritated with me."

The extraction took ten minutes. It felt like ten hours. Ysolde worked with meticulous precision, her forceps probing the wound track, finding the fragments of rotten timber buried in my calf muscle and withdrawing them one by one — eight pieces, ranging from the size of a matchstick to a splinter the length of my little finger that came out trailing a thin streamer of dark blood.

I held still. I did not scream. I may have whispered some words that would have gotten me banned from every temple in the city, but I did not scream.

When the last fragment came out, Ysolde irrigated the wound with the clear liquid again, then applied the amber solution — warm and viscous, with a numbing quality that spread through the wounded tissue like a blessing. The pain didn't disappear, but it retreated, pulling back from the screaming foreground to a dull, distant throb.

She stitched the wound closed — twelve neat sutures, each one placed with the care of a seamstress working on a wedding dress. Then she wrapped it in clean linen, firm but not tight, and turned her attention to my ankle.

The ankle got a different treatment. She manipulated the joint — gently but thoroughly, testing range of motion, pressing on specific points that made me wince and one that made me nearly come off the table. Then she applied a poultice — something green and foul-smelling that she packed around the swollen joint and wrapped in place with linen strips.

"Severe sprain," she said. "Partial ligament tear. It will heal in two to three weeks if you stay off it. If you insist on walking, wrap it tightly and use a staff."

"And the calf?"

"The infection risk is the concern. The wood was rotten — old mine timber, I assume. Full of microbial contamination." She turned to the shelf and selected a small glass vial — the green one. The liquid inside was dark, almost black-green, and seemed to shift when she tilted it, as if something inside was moving. "Drink this."

I looked at the vial. It looked back.

"What is it?"

"A restorative compound. Anti-infectious, anti-inflammatory, promotes tissue regeneration. My own formulation."

"What's in it?"

"Things you wouldn't recognize if I named them." She held it out. Patient. Unhurried. "You can refuse, of course. And you can walk out of here with a wound that will fester within twenty-four hours. At which point you'll need a temple healer — one who will charge you ten times what I'm charging and will probably still take the leg."

I looked at Mira. She shrugged — a gesture that communicated both *I don't know* and *what choice do you have?*

I took the vial. Uncorked it. The smell was... strange. Not unpleasant, exactly. Sharp and green, like fresh-cut herbs, but underneath that, something else — something metallic, almost electric, like the air before a lightning storm.

I drank it.

The taste hit me first — intensely bitter, then sweet, then a warmth that spread from my throat down into my chest like swallowing liquid sunlight. It wasn't bad. It was just... a lot. A lot of flavor, a lot of sensation, a lot of *something* that I couldn't name moving through me.

Ysolde watched me drink with an expression I couldn't read. Not concern. Not satisfaction. Something else — something patient and evaluating, like a gardener watching a seed she'd just planted.

"Good," she said. "The effects will begin within twenty-four hours. You'll feel warmth, possibly tingling. That's normal. Your body is fighting the infection." She took the empty vial back and placed it on her shelf. "Eight coppers."

"Eight coppers?"

"For the extraction, the suturing, the poultice, and the compound. I told you I don't charge much."

I paid her. It took most of my remaining coin, but after what she'd done — the precision, the care, the numbness that was currently making my leg feel almost human again — it felt like a bargain.

Mira helped me off the table. My ankle protested but held, especially with Mira's shoulder to lean on.

At the door, I paused. Something was nagging at me. Something about the way Ysolde had watched me drink the vial. The patience in it. The attention.

"The compound," I said. "You said the effects begin within twenty-four hours."

"That's correct."

"What effects, exactly? Beyond fighting the infection?"

She looked at me with those dark, depthless eyes. And she smiled — a small, closed-lipped expression that didn't quite reach her eyes and didn't quite answer the question.

"Healing," she said. "The body knows what it needs. The compound simply... facilitates."

I didn't like that answer. I didn't like the smile, either. But my leg hurt, and I was tired, and the numbness from the amber solution was making everything feel soft and distant, and Mira was already steering me toward the door.

"Rest tonight," Ysolde called after us. "Sleep well. And come back if you need anything."

The door closed behind us.

---

We made it back to the Broken Antler just before dawn. The eastern sky was going gray-pink over the rooftops, the city slowly waking around us — bakers' smoke, cart wheels, the distant clang of the harbor bell. The tavern's front door was unlocked — it always was — and the innkeeper barely looked up from his ledger as Mira hauled me through the common room and up the stairs to our room.

She helped me onto the bed. My bed, the one nearest the window, with the mattress that was more lump than cushion and the blanket that smelled like it had been washed sometime during the previous monarchy. It was the most comfortable thing I'd ever felt.

"We didn't get the crates," I said.

"We didn't die. That's worth more than the crates."

"Grent's going to want his advance back."

"Grent can find us. We'll deal with it tomorrow." She pulled my boots off — the intact one and the one she'd already removed to examine my ankle — and covered me with the blanket. "Sleep. I'll watch for a while."

"You don't need to—"

"Sleep, Aldric."

I slept.

My last thought before the darkness took me was about the vial. The way the liquid had looked — alive, almost. Shifting. And the way Ysolde had smiled when I asked about the effects.

*Healing,* she'd said. *The body knows what it needs.*

I dreamed about water. About moonlight. About looking into a mirror and seeing someone I didn't recognize looking back.

---

## DAY ZERO — The Morning Everything Changed

I woke up feeling... strange.

Not bad. Not in pain — which was itself strange, because I'd gone to sleep with a torn ankle and a perforated calf and both of those things should have been screaming at me the moment consciousness arrived. But the pain was muted, distant, like hearing thunder from very far away. A background hum rather than a foreground roar.

I lay there for a moment, staring at the ceiling of our room at the Broken Antler. Cracks in the plaster. A water stain shaped vaguely like a horse. Morning light filtering through the window, pale gold, the color of watered honey. Normal. Everything normal.

Except.

My skin felt... different. I rubbed my arm against the rough blanket and the sensation was sharper than it should have been. Not painful — just *more*. Like the nerve endings had been turned up a notch. The weave of the blanket, the texture of the linen — I could feel every thread.

I sat up. Mira was asleep in the chair by the door, her head tilted back, her bow across her lap. She'd meant to keep watch and had lost the fight with exhaustion sometime before dawn. I didn't blame her. She'd carried me through a goblin mine, across a forest, and through the streets of Valdmere's worst district. She'd earned the rest.

I swung my legs out of bed — carefully, expecting the ankle to punish me. It hurt, but manageable. The poultice Ysolde had packed around it had dried into a stiff cast-like shell that actually supported the joint. The calf wound throbbed under its bandages, but the angry heat of incipient infection was gone. Whatever was in that green vial, it was doing its job.

I stood. Hobbled to the small mirror on the wall — a cheap thing, polished tin rather than proper glass, that showed a warped, imprecise version of my face.

I looked at myself. Square jaw. Stubble. Green eyes. The scar through my left eyebrow. Everything where it should be. Everything the same.

So why did I feel like something was different?

I touched my face. My cheek. The skin under my fingers was... softer? Or was I imagining it? I pressed harder. Same jaw. Same bone structure. Same face I'd been wearing for twenty-six years.

Imagination. Exhaustion. After-effects of whatever was in that vial.

I turned away from the mirror.

Behind me, Mira stirred. "You're up."

"I'm up."

"How's the leg?"

"Better than it should be."

She opened her eyes, stretched in the chair, winced at whatever the night had done to her neck. "The witch's medicine?"

"Ysolde."

"Whatever. Does the leg work?"

I tested it. Careful steps across the room. The ankle complained — loudly — but it bore weight. The calf was stiff and sore but the wound wasn't burning anymore. I'd need a walking staff, and I wouldn't be running anytime soon, but I could move.

"Good enough," I said.

Mira stood and went to the window. Morning light caught her face, the amber eyes, the cropped copper hair. She looked tired. She looked like someone who'd killed eight goblins and carried a full-grown man through a forest and was very much regretting her career choices.

"We need food," she said. "And we need a plan. We owe Grent for the advance, we've got no cargo to deliver, and you're walking like a three-legged dog."

"I'm walking."

"Barely."

She was right. About all of it. We were worse off than yesterday — no cargo, an angry client, and my leg was a liability that would take weeks to heal properly. We needed money. We needed work. We needed something to go right for once.

But under all of that — beneath the practical concerns and the financial anxiety and the dull ache in my leg — something else nagged at me. Something I couldn't quite put into words. A feeling, vague and formless, sitting in the back of my mind like a splinter in soft wood.

The vial. Ysolde's smile. The way the liquid had moved inside the glass, almost alive.

*The body knows what it needs. The compound simply facilitates.*

Facilitates what?

I shook it off. Got dressed. Went downstairs for breakfast. The day began.

Tomorrow — *Day One* — I would wake up and the mirror would start telling me things I wasn't ready to hear.

But I didn't know that yet.
